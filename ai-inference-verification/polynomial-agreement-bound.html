Polynomial Agreement Bound

Polynomials · agreement points

Two different polynomials of degree below d agree on at most d − 1 points.

You’ll also see this written as “at most d points.” That’s true, but it’s one more than needed. Two different lines have degree below 2, and they cross at most once, not twice.

1 shared point most possible: d − 1 = 1

Why it’s true

  1. Subtract. Let h = f − g. Because f ≠ g, h is not the zero polynomial. Subtracting can’t raise the degree, so h also has degree below d.

  2. Agreement means a root. If f(r) = g(r), then h(r) = 0. The points where f and g agree are exactly the roots of h.

  3. Count the roots. Each root r splits off a factor (x − r). With k different roots, h contains (x − r1)···(x − rk), which has degree k. That can’t exceed the degree of h, which is at most d − 1. So h has at most d − 1 roots, and f and g agree at most d − 1 times.

Read it the other way

Through d points with different x-values there is at most one polynomial of degree below d, because two of them would agree on d points. Lagrange interpolation shows there is always exactly one. Scene 4 shows this.

Not only for curves

The proof uses factoring, never the picture, so it holds over any field, including the finite fields used in Reed–Solomon codes, secret sharing and polynomial commitments. Pick r at random from q field elements and two different polynomials of degree below d agree at r with probability at most (d − 1)/q.
