The One-Cell Amplifier

Low-degree encoding · distance

Change one cell of the tape and the curve through the cells changes almost everywhere.

This is the agreement bound put to work. The old tape and the new tape give two different polynomials of degree below n, so they agree on at most n − 1 points. Read the curve at far more than n places and nearly every reading differs. A one-cell change becomes a change you can’t miss.

Why one cell turns into almost all of them

  1. Encode. Put the tape’s n cells at x = 0, 1, …, n − 1 and take the one polynomial of degree below n that passes through them. Write down its value at N points, with N much larger than n. On this page n = 16, N = 256, and the arithmetic is mod 257.

  2. Change one cell. The new tape has its own polynomial, also of degree below n, and it’s a different polynomial from the old one because it passes through a different value at that cell.

  3. Count where they still agree. Two different polynomials of degree below n agree on at most n − 1 points. The n − 1 untouched cells already use up that budget, so every other reading changes: at least N − (n − 1) of them, which is 241 of 256 here. The same count holds for any change to the tape, not just one cell.

Why it’s called an amplifier

On the raw tape, a single wrong cell is a needle in a haystack: a checker reading random cells needs about n tries to find it. After encoding, the same lie shows up in nearly every reading, so a handful of random reads finds it. The proof only ever looks at a few spots, and this is what makes a few spots enough.

The other half of the job

A cheater could also hand over a table that isn’t the readings of any low-degree curve at all. A separate low-degree test checks for that. Once the table is known to be close to some curve, the amplification here takes over.
