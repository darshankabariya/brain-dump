1 · The problem

You are sold one model. How do you know that is the one running?

Start with a plain example. Your company pays a reseller for answers from a large model. The model runs in the reseller's datacentre. A smaller or compressed copy would cost them much less to run, and its answers would read almost the same. You have no way to tell which one answered you.

SASH's own training material points out that three different people have this same problem. Pick one:

The problem in one sentence: someone makes a claim about a computation you did not run, on hardware you do not control, and they will not hand you their secrets.

So what is SASH?

  • It is not a regulator. The Singapore AI Safety Hub is an independent nonprofit that launched in Singapore in early 2025. It does research and builds the AI-safety field in Asia. It has no power over any AI lab.
  • It is not a company with a product. As far as I can find, it sells nothing. Its verification team builds prototype tools in public and shows them to policymakers.
  • Who the tools are for: governments that want to sign AI agreements they can check (it names smaller "middle power" countries in particular), safety teams, and companies in high-stakes industries that buy AI.
  • Its own description of the work: "Tools and protocols that prove whether AI systems meet their safety claims across training, evaluation and deployment." Its About page names Pascal Berrang as the lead of its technical AI verification work.

So think of SASH as a workshop that builds measuring instruments. Regulators, inspectors and buyers would be the ones who use them. SASH is never inside the transaction itself:

RoleWho plays it
ProverWhoever runs the model: the lab, or the hosting provider
VerifierYou: the buyer, the safety team, the inspector, or an auditor you appoint
Tool builderSASH. It builds the open prototype, measures what it costs, and shows policymakers what is possible
2 · When the check happens

At launch or on every prompt? Both. They are different claims.

A model owner makes different promises at different moments. Each promise needs its own tool. This is the map, in time order, with the tool SASH or Berrang is prototyping for each.

Before launch
This model scored X on this safety test. The test runs inside a sealed hardware enclave, so the lab keeps its weights private and the test owner keeps the questions private. A signed receipt comes out. Tool: hardware attestation · SASH's evaluation receipt registry
At launch
The model we deployed is the one that was tested. A proof that two models are the same, without showing either. Tool: model equality in zero-knowledge · a project Berrang mentors
Every request
This answer came from the declared model. Route A, recompute: an auditor re-runs a random sample of logged requests on its own copy and compares. Cheap. But the auditor must hold the weights, and the operator writes the log. Route B, prove: the operator attaches a proof to the answer. The checker needs only a fingerprint of the weights, never the weights. Tool for route B: Berrang's zk-inference-prover
For all behaviour
This system can never break this rule. A statement about every possible run, where the other three are about one test or one answer. It is the furthest from practice. Tool: zero-knowledge model checking · the paper this page explains

Route B in practice: buying through a reseller

Take the buyer again. You call a reseller's API and pay for a large model. This is how a proof would travel, and what happens when the model is swapped.

What is missing today

The prototype cannot do the reseller case yet. Four gaps stand between it and that picture:

  • Models. It proves one open model, Llama-3.1-8B, in a fixed quantized form. No closed model is supported.
  • Hiding. I found no zero-knowledge blinding in the prover code. An owner of secret weights would need it.
  • Link to the API call. The repo's own gap document says a proof is not yet tied to what was served. It proves token ids, not the text you sent and received, and it names no request or time.
  • Cost. Section 3 covers this.

On billing, which is my own reasoning and not a use SASH states: a proof makes the prompt tokens and output tokens public, so a buyer could count proved tokens and compare them with an invoice. It would not show the price is fair, and today nothing links a proof to an invoice.

Is every prompt really proved?

It can be, one request at a time. The open question is cost, and measuring that cost is what the prover repo is for. On one H100 it proves about 1.25 token positions per second, roughly $0.73 per 1,000 positions. SASH's own notes put that between about 30 and 1,000 times slower than plain serving, depending on how much of the request is prompt.

SASH's own job posting says a verifier does not need every answer proved. Checking a random sample of about 300 outputs is enough for 95% confidence that 99% of the compute is used legitimately. So the realistic picture is a proof for a sample of requests, not for each one.

Why not simply look inside?

Every row above runs into the same wall. The weights, or the design, are the owner's secret. That leaves three options.

Open the boxHand over the weights or the design. The checker can re-run everything. The secret is gone. Route A needs this.
Say "trust us"Keep everything private. The checker has only the owner's word.
Seal the proofKeep everything private and hand over a proof that anyone can check. This is where zero-knowledge comes in.

The next section looks at what proving costs. After that, the page takes the hardest claim, the last row: a rule that holds on every run. Proving that needs formal verification, which produces the proof, and zero-knowledge, which lets someone check it without seeing the machine. You already know the second. The next section gives you the first.

3 · The cost

Why proving is slow: everything has to be written down

First, how a model is run

A language model produces one token at a time. Each step reads every token so far, gives a score to every word in its vocabulary (128,256 words for Llama 3), and one is chosen. The chosen token is added to the text and the step repeats. Step through one:

Two phases matter for cost. The prompt is read in one parallel pass, called prefill: the GPU handles all 4,096 prompt tokens at once. Then each new token needs its own pass, called decode, which reuses a cache of what the earlier tokens computed. That is why serving is very fast on prompts and slower on generation.

The repos run the model in two ways, and the prover is a third:

  • HF Transformers is the Hugging Face library most research code uses: a plain PyTorch loop, easy to read and change. The harness uses it to run the model in fixed arithmetic and dump every intermediate value, which is the material the proof is built from.
  • vLLM is a serving engine. It batches many requests, pages the cache, and fuses GPU kernels. It is what a provider runs, and it is the "plain serving" the prover is compared with.
  • The prover pays the same for every position, prompt or generated, because every position's intermediate values must be written down and committed.

Where the time goes

The natural guess is that the STARK or the Merkle trees are the slow part. The measurements say otherwise. Formal verification is not involved at all: the inference prover contains none.

  • The STARK is under 5%. Merkle trees and FRI are already fast on a GPU, which commits about 2 billion field elements per second.
  • It used to be the bottleneck. With constraint evaluation left on the CPU, the same sample spends 3,984 seconds in the proof system. With generated GPU kernels it spends 34. That optimisation is done.
  • What is left is preparing the data. Building the trace and the lookup columns is 77% of the time, and part of it still runs on the CPU. The prover's issue list names this as its ceiling.

The root cause

Normal inference does the arithmetic and throws the intermediate values away. A proof has to write every one of them down as a field element, plus extra columns for each rounding, range check and table lookup. That comes to about 79 million numbers for one token position. Attention makes it grow with context: about 105 million per position at 16,384 tokens.

Why the gap is a range and not one number

Proving costs the same for every position. Serving does not, because a GPU reads all the prompt tokens in one parallel pass and then generates one token at a time. So a request that is mostly prompt looks far worse than one that is mostly generation. For this request, a 4,096-token prompt with 512 generated tokens, the measured gap is about 1,070 times.

How much faster could it get?

The repo carries two projections for this request, which takes 61 minutes to prove today:

  • About 30 minutes if the phases still on the CPU gain what the already-moved phases measured.
  • About 12 minutes if all remaining CPU work became 8 times faster. Nothing has measured that factor; the repo labels it an assumption.

My arithmetic on top: even at 12 minutes, proving this request would still be roughly 200 times slower than serving it. Past that point the STARK itself, about 5 minutes for this request, becomes the floor.

4 · Formal verification as a black box

Two things go in, a verdict with evidence comes out

Take a controller whose design is secret, and a rule it must never break. Skip the theory for now and treat formal verification as a box.

Input 1A machine: states, and arrows between them
Input 2A rule: "never wait forever"
Verifierchecks every run
Yesand here is a certificate you can check yourself
Noand here is the exact run that breaks the rule

The important word is every. Testing tries some runs. Verification covers all of them, including runs nobody thought to try. The figure below shows the difference. It is a simplified version of the paper's own example: a TCP-style handshake that sends syn, waits for synack, and retries up to three times.

What you just saw

  • Tests sample runs. The buggy design passes 200 out of 200, because in a random test the reply usually arrives. The one run that matters, where the reply never arrives, goes on forever and so never reports anything.
  • The countdown covers all runs at once. Give every state a number. If every arrow leads to a smaller number, no run can go on forever, because a whole number cannot keep dropping below zero.
  • That numbering is the certificate. The paper calls it a ranking function. Finding one can be hard. Checking one is easy: look at each arrow once.

Hard to find and easy to check: that is the same shape as a witness in a ZK proof. This is why the two fields fit together.

5 · The paper's main move

Checking the countdown needs the arrows, and the arrows are the secret

To check a countdown you look at every arrow. The regulator can see the numbers. It cannot see the arrows. The paper turns the question around:

  1. The numbers are public. From them, anyone can list every arrow that would break the rule: every pair of states where the number fails to drop. Call this the forbidden list.
  2. The owner commits to its real arrows with a hiding commitment.
  3. The owner proves one statement: none of my committed arrows is on the forbidden list.

In your vocabulary: RLN proves "my key is in the membership set". This proves "none of my arrows is in the forbidden set". It is a non-membership proof over a hidden set.

Under the hood, in terms you know

The arrows are encoded as a polynomial that equals 1 at every real arrow and 0 at every other pair of states. One batched KZG opening shows that this committed polynomial is zero at every forbidden point. The proof is two group elements, whatever the size of the machine. The prover's work grows with the square of the number of states, so this version reaches about 4,000 states (212) inside the paper's two-hour limit.

One honest weakness, which the paper names itself: the public numbers hint at the shape of the secret machine. In the grid above, the checker learns that no arrow sits in a hatched cell. How much a public certificate leaks is listed as an open problem.

6 · The second scheme

When the machine is too big to draw

Real systems are written as rules, and a dozen rules can stand for millions of states. For example: if waiting and delay ≥ 1, then delay goes down by 1. The secret is now the numbers inside the rules, written as matrices.

The countdown check turns into statements of this kind: there is no state where this rule fires and the number fails to drop. Each one says that a group of linear inequalities has no solution. That sounds like checking every state. An old result called Farkas' lemma avoids it: when inequalities contradict each other, there is a recipe of multipliers that adds them up into something plainly false.

So each check becomes a normal ZK statement: I know multipliers that make these committed matrices add up to a contradiction. The paper proves it with Pedersen commitments, matrix-product proofs and range proofs. A real protocol produces thousands of checks with the same shape, so they are folded into one, in the spirit of Nova.

7 · The bigger picture

What Berrang is trying to do

This section is my reading of the public record. I could not find one sentence where he states it himself, so treat the framing as mine.

One goal: let an outsider check a claim about an AI system while the owner keeps the system private. His projects differ in which claim they prove.

Where he sits

  • University of Birmingham. Associate Professor in Computer Security. His earlier work was on privacy attacks against ML models (membership inference, ML-Leaks).
  • Zeroth Research. Co-founder. His co-author Mirco Giacobbe is its CEO. It builds formal methods and cryptographic verification tools for AI.
  • Funding named in the paper. ARIA's Safeguarded AI programme and an Amazon Research Award.
  • SASH. An independent nonprofit, and no regulator. Its About page names him as the lead of its technical AI verification work. Its other public repos take the routes from section 2: re-running inference to compare, and hardware attestation for evaluations. Zero-knowledge is one route among several there, and the prover is his.
8 · What kind of work

Research or optimisation? Both, on different projects

The paperA new idea at toy scale

It is the first scheme of its kind. The open questions it names:

  • What a public rule and a public certificate leak about the secret machine.
  • What it means for security to commit before or after the proof.
  • Probabilistic and continuous systems, which are closer to real AI and robotics and need new cryptographic routines.
  • Other folding designs, and a Groth16 or PlonK version with constant-size proofs and a slower prover.

The inference proverEngineering and measurement

The idea is fixed. The work is making it sound, auditable, broader and faster. From its open issues:

  • Split the circuit into small gadgets that can be audited and tested one at a time (#28 and its sub-issues).
  • Support a second architecture. Qwen cannot be proved today because of a missing bias term (#27, #10).
  • Move the rest of witness generation onto the GPU. The STARK itself is under 5% of proving time, as section 3 shows (#2).
  • Tests that try to cheat the verifier (#3, #5).
  • Memory at long context (#7, #12), and a 70B campaign in the results repo.

Where it is probably heading

This is inference from the issue lists and the paper's conclusion. I have no inside knowledge of his plans.

  • Prover: bigger models, more architectures, and closing the gap between what is proved (greedy decoding of one request) and what a provider really serves (sampling, batching, chat templates).
  • Model checker: richer kinds of systems. The GitHub organisation that hosts the prototype also hosts a repo named neuralmc, and the paper cites Giacobbe's "Neural model checking", where, as I understand it, a neural network plays the role of the countdown. Combining that with zero-knowledge would be a natural next step. That is a guess.
9 · Before you write to him

Where your background meets his work

Underconstrained circuits

You wrote that a circuit can pass every test and still be underconstrained. The prover's gadget refactor is about exactly this: one row-level constraint test per gadget (#29) and negative controls (#3). Your Lean and Clean learning points the same way.

Groth16 in production

The paper says its core relation could run directly on Groth16 or PlonK. You have maintained a Groth16 system in a live network through RLN.

Membership proofs

RLN proves membership against an on-chain root. The explicit scheme proves non-membership with KZG. Same family, opposite direction.

Benchmarks and CI

The prover pins a proof digest on two backends before any merge. You added proving benchmarks and a daily end-to-end RLN test.

Questions you could honestly ask

  1. For the model checker, which open problem matters most to you now: what the public certificate leaks, or reaching probabilistic systems?
  2. Would a Groth16 or PlonK version of the symbolic scheme be useful to anyone yet, or is prover time the only thing that matters at this stage?
  3. In the inference prover, how do you gain confidence that a gadget is not underconstrained? Is formal verification of the gadgets themselves on the table?
  4. Do you see the model checker and the inference prover meeting, for example a proof that a served model obeys a safety rule?

Pick the one you most want answered. A short message with one real question does more than a summary of his own work.
